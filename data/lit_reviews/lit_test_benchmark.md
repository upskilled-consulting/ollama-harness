## Overview

The literature review on LLM evaluation benchmarks reveals a comprehensive approach to assessing the performance of large language models across diverse tasks. It highlights the development of multiple benchmarks, such as JETTS, JudgeSense, IF-RewardBench, Agent-as-a-Judge, and M-JudgeBench, which are designed to evaluate judge models in various contexts. ## 1. LLM Evaluation Benchmarks

The research papers collectively focus on developing and evaluating judge models for large language models (LLMs) across various domains, including math reasoning, code generation, instruction following, and automated AI development tasks. They introduce multiple benchmarks such as JETTS, JudgeSense, IF-RewardBench, Agent-as-a-Judge, and M-JudgeBench, which assess the performance of judge models in different contexts and against diverse criteria. These studies highlight the strengths and weaknesses of current judge models, revealing that while they can be competitive with outcome reward models, they often underperform in step-level beam search procedures and struggle to differentiate between model abilities, particularly when faced with complex or multimodal tasks.

### Evaluating Judges as Evaluators: The JETTS Benchmark of LLM-as-Judges as Test-Time Scaling Evaluators

*[2504.15253v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15253v2) · 2025-04-21 · cited ×3 · score 10.0/10*

**Topic**: This paper investigates the effectiveness of LLM-as-judges, which generate natural language evaluations, when used as evaluators for test-time scaling of large language models. It addresses the gap in understanding how these generative judges compare to traditional non-generative reward models in inference-time settings.

**Motivation**: While LLM-judges are becoming popular for automatic evaluation, their specific utility in test-time scaling scenarios remains largely unknown despite their empirical successes in other contexts. The research is motivated by the need to determine if these models can effectively guide generators during inference compared to established external evaluators.

**Contribution**: The authors introduce the Judge Evaluation for Test-Time Scaling (JETTS) benchmark to assess judge performance across three domains: math reasoning, code generation, and instruction following. They evaluate ten judge models (7B-70B parameters) against eight base generator models (6.7B-72B parameters) under three task settings: response reranking, step-level beam search, and critique-based response refinement.

**Evidence**: Results indicate that judges are competitive with outcome reward models in response reranking tasks but are consistently outperformed by process reward models in step-level beam search procedures. This highlights a performance disparity depending on the specific test-time scaling method employed.

**Narrow impact**: The findings are specific to the performance of LLM-judges within the context of test-time scaling for math, code, and instruction-following tasks. It clarifies the limitations of using judges for process-level guidance (beam search) and refinement compared to outcome-level evaluation (reranking).

**Broad impact**: This work provides a standardized framework for evaluating the role of LLM-judges in test-time computation, helping to delineate where they are suitable substitutes for reward models and where they fall short. It informs the development of more robust automatic evaluation systems by identifying the current ineffectiveness of natural language critiques in complex scaling tasks.

**Limitations**: The natural language critiques generated by LLM-judges are found to be currently ineffective in guiding the generator toward better responses. This suggests that, unlike their performance in simple reranking, the explanatory output of judges does not yet provide actionable value for response refinement.

### JudgeSense: A Benchmark for Prompt Sensitivity in LLM-as-a-Judge Systems

*[2604.23478v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23478v1) · 2026-04-26 · score 10.0/10*

**Topic**: The paper investigates the stability of large language models when deployed as automated judges, specifically focusing on how their verdicts change under semantically equivalent prompt paraphrases.

**Motivation**: As LLMs become common evaluators, their reliability is compromised if their decisions are unstable across different phrasings of the same content, yet this sensitivity remains unmeasured.

**Contribution**: The authors introduce JudgeSense, a framework and benchmark that quantifies prompt sensitivity using the Judge Sensitivity Score (JSS), defined as the fraction of paraphrase pairs yielding identical decisions.

**Evidence**: On factuality, judges cluster near a JSS of 0.63 due to a polarity-inverted prompt artifact, but this rises to about 0.9 after correction, while pairwise tasks show degenerate always-A behavior in 8 of 9 judges due to strong position bias.

**Narrow impact**: The release of code, decision logs, and a validated paraphrase dataset supports standardized reporting of JSS for specific evaluation tasks like factuality and preference.

**Broad impact**: This work provides a necessary tool for assessing the reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge systems, potentially improving the trustworthiness of automated evaluation in AI research.

**Limitations**: Model scale does not predict consistency, indicating that larger models are not inherently more stable judges regardless of their size.

**Narrow impact**: The findings specifically apply to the evaluation of mathematical and complex reasoning tasks where exact answers and specific formats (like proofs or images) are required. It shows that for these domains, dataset quality and judge reliability are critical bottlenecks that must be addressed to accurately assess model performance.

**Broad impact**: The work suggests that benchmark saturation may be an artifact of noisy data and unreliable judges rather than inherent model limits. **Limitations**: Neither the improved judge nor the original judge identifies the present failure modes for the subset of tagged problems. This demonstrates that even with better data, current evaluation methods fail to recognize specific types of model errors, limiting their diagnostic utility.
